# Luxury Relevance Index

How relevant is luxury today, and is what people value in luxury changing? An independent student project using open, aggregated data.

## Questions
1. Is interest in luxury rising or declining?
2. Is the way luxury is covered and discussed changing?
3. Is there a shift from status toward craftsmanship, heritage, or experience? (a hypothesis to test)
4. Do luxury segments and example brands show different patterns?
5. Are there early signals of changing preferences?
6. Which strategies are worth investigating for brands?

## Status

Analysis complete. An experimental relevance index and a dashboard are in progress. Full results, methods and limits are in FINDINGS.md. Sources are in SOURCES.md.

## Conclusions

Public attention data gives a mixed picture of luxury's relevance. Our own data measures attention, not what consumers value or buy, so published survey evidence does most of the work on question 3.

| Question | Conclusion | Main basis | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Is interest rising or declining? | Mixed | Google searches for four luxury terms were higher in 2025-2026 than in 2019-2024. Wikipedia readership for the luxury brands fell, but unrelated control articles fell by a similar amount, so most of that fall is site-wide. Hermes held up on both sources, while Gucci fell on searches. | Attention is not sales. A shared jump across the four search terms in August 2025 is unexplained. |
| 2. Is the way luxury is discussed changing? | Partly | "Quiet luxury" went from near zero to a sustained search term, with a clear step in April 2023. | No measure of news coverage or tone over time, because news-data access was rate-limited. |
| 3. Is there a shift from status toward craftsmanship, heritage or experience? | Partly supported | Published surveys and reports point away from logos and toward quality, emotional connection and experiences. One survey shows status associations remain widespread. | Surveys are snapshots of stated preferences. Our own data cannot show what people value. |
| 4. Do segments and brands differ? | Brands partly, segments not | The four search-term segments move mostly as one series. Hermes behaves differently from Gucci and Louis Vuitton. | Three brands only. No audience data per brand. |
| 5. Are there early signals? | Not supported | No consistent lead between Wikipedia attention and Google searches, and unrelated control pairs showed similar correlations. Forecasts beat simple baselines only slightly, and only about one month ahead. | Monthly data and few real events. |
| 6. Which strategies are worth investigating? | Hypotheses only | See below. | Our data cannot test whether a strategy works. |

## Strategies worth investigating (hypotheses, not recommendations)

These come from the cited reports, not from our own tests. A brand with its own sales and customer data could test them.

1. Lead with quality, design and discreet codes rather than loud logos.
2. Invest in experiences and emotional connection, which surveys rank highly.
3. Review price increases, which reports link to weaker demand.
4. Develop resale and repair, especially for established customers.
5. Focus on top-tier clients, whose share of spend has grown.

## What this project can and cannot say

It can show how public attention moved, and which popular readings of it are misleading. For example, a fall in Wikipedia views looks like lost interest until unrelated articles are shown to have fallen too. It cannot say what consumers think or buy, or why, and it does not predict sales. News coverage, consumer text and brand-level sales were not available to us.
